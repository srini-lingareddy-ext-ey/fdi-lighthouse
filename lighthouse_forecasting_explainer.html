Step 01 of 06

Build the Business Foundation

Lighthouse begins with the same data finance leaders already trust — historical financial performance and the operational and market signals that influence results.

  • Historical financial accounts (volume, revenue, costs, margins)
  • Internal business drivers (pricing, backlog, production plans)
  • External drivers (economic indicators, industry trends, demand signals)
  • Business hierarchies: product, region, and segment groupings
Why This Matters

Forecasts are grounded in real business structure — not abstract models — making results immediately usable for FP&A, operations, and leadership discussions.

Step 02 of 06

Identify the Drivers That Truly Matter

Rather than assuming what drives results, Lighthouse systematically determines which internal and external factors actually influence each financial account.

  • Evaluates a broad candidate pool of drivers per account
  • Runs multiple ranking algorithms simultaneously for robustness
  • Removes duplicates (collinearity) so each driver adds unique signal
  • Different accounts can and do have different drivers
Why This Matters

Finance teams move from "best guesses" to evidence-based forecasting, improving confidence and reducing debate over assumptions in every planning cycle.

⚙ Technical Detail: Multi-Algorithm Driver Ranking

Lighthouse does not rely on a single metric. It runs the driver ranking step through multiple methods, then aggregates scores for a consensus ranking.

⚙ Technical Detail: mRMR — Minimum Redundancy Maximum Relevance

The mRMR algorithm addresses a classic pitfall: selecting many drivers that all measure the same thing. It maximises relevance to the target account while minimising redundancy between selected drivers.

Step 03 of 06

Understand When Drivers Take Effect

Business impacts don't always happen immediately. A contract signed today may not appear in revenue for three months. Lighthouse explicitly accounts for timing and delay between cause and effect.

  • Tests every driver at lag 0 through n_max_lag periods
  • Identifies the lag that maximises predictive power per driver
  • Prioritises lags that reduce reliance on uncertain future inputs
  • Validates that time-series length supports the chosen lag depth
Why This Matters

Forecasts align with how the business actually behaves over time — not how a spreadsheet assumes it behaves. A lagged driver also often means the signal is available before the financial impact occurs, improving real-time forecast accuracy.

⚙ Technical Detail: Lag Selection Algorithm 
# lh_v2/driver_analysis/lagging/lag_handling.py

def select_best_lags(info, da_params):
    """
    For every driver d and every lag k in [0 … n_max_lag]:
      1. Shift d backward by k periods
      2. Run the ranking algorithm on the shifted driver
      3. Record the rank score for (d, k)
    Then: choose the highest lag among the top-N ranked
    (d, k) pairs — preferring longer lags to maximise the
    look-ahead advantage in real-time forecasting.
    """
    best_lags = {}
    for driver in all_drivers:
        scores = {}
        for k in range(0, da_params.lag_params.n_max_lag + 1):
            shifted = shift_driver(driver, lag=k)
            scores[k] = rank_single(shifted, account)
        top_k = top_n(scores, n=da_params.lag_params.n_top_considered)
        best_lags[driver] = max(top_k)   # prefer longer lags
    return best_lags

Constraint: time series must be at least n_max_lag + 2 periods long to allow proper cross-validation after shifting.

⚙ Technical Detail: Why Long Lags Are Preferred

When two lags (e.g. lag-2 and lag-5) perform equally well, Lighthouse selects lag-5. This is a deliberate design choice: a driver at lag-5 means that by the time a forecast is needed, the driver value for the relevant historical period is already known and recorded — eliminating a source of forecast error.

# Practical example: Industrial Output Index
#   Lag 0 → score 0.78  (requires current-month data)
#   Lag 1 → score 0.80  (last month's data — available)
#   Lag 3 → score 0.80  (3-months-ago data — firmly available)
#   Lag 5 → score 0.79  (5-months-ago — maximum look-ahead)
#
# top_n=3 selects lags {1, 3, 5} → best_lag = 5
# Using Q-3 Industrial Output to forecast current revenue
# means the driver value is always known and verified.

Step 04 of 06

Select the Best Forecast Approach Automatically

Instead of forcing one forecasting method across the entire business, Lighthouse selects the most reliable approach for each individual account independently.

  • Trains multiple models per account in parallel
  • Evaluates accuracy using walk-forward cross-validation
  • Uses finance-standard error metrics (MAPE, MAE, RMSE)
  • Hyperparameter optimisation via Bayesian search for top candidates
Why This Matters

Forecast quality improves without requiring analysts to manually choose or tune models. High-volume stable accounts and volatile low-volume accounts receive methods tailored to their statistical profile.

⚙ Technical Detail: Forecasting Method Library

# Model selection via walk-forward cross-validation
def select_best_model(account_ts, drivers, cv_params):
    results = {}
    for method, Model in FORECASTING_METHODS.items():
        cv_score = walk_forward_cv(Model, account_ts, drivers)
        results[method] = cv_score
    return min(results, key=lambda m: results[m]['MAPE'])
⚙ Technical Detail: Bayesian Hyperparameter Optimisation

For models with tunable parameters (e.g. XGBoost, SARIMAX), Lighthouse uses Bayesian optimisation (via HyperOpt) to efficiently search the parameter space — far more efficient than grid search.

# lh_v2/forecasting/account_forecasting/hyperparameter_optimization/
# lh_v2/full_pipeline_scripts/script_hyperopt.py

from hyperopt import fmin, tpe, hp

search_space = {
    "n_estimators": hp.quniform("n_estimators", 50, 500, 10),
    "max_depth":    hp.choice("max_depth", [3, 4, 5, 6, 8]),
    "learning_rate":hp.loguniform("lr", -4, -1),   # [0.018, 0.368]
    "subsample":    hp.uniform("sub", 0.6, 1.0),
}

best = fmin(
    fn=lambda params: cv_loss(XGBoostModel(**params), account_ts),
    space=search_space,
    algo=tpe.suggest,            # Tree-structured Parzen Estimator
    max_evals=100,
)

Step 05 of 06

Reconcile Forecasts to Financial Reality

Independent model forecasts frequently violate accounting identities. Revenue minus COGS should equal Gross Profit — but when each account is modelled separately, this relationship rarely holds exactly. Lighthouse enforces it automatically.

  • Applies algebraic formulas (e.g. Revenue − COGS = Gross Profit)
  • Optionally uses MINT adjustment for system-wide optimisation
  • Falls back gracefully when MINT prerequisites are not met
  • Full audit trail of every reconciliation adjustment
Why This Matters

Leadership receives forecasts that tie out cleanly, reducing rework, questions, and downstream corrections in the planning cycle.

⚙ Technical Detail: MINT Reconciliation Theory

MINT (Minimum Trace) reconciliation — from Wickramasuriya et al. (2019) — finds the linear combination of base forecasts that minimises forecast variance while satisfying all hierarchical constraints simultaneously. This is mathematically superior to post-hoc formula application when forecasts exist at multiple levels.

def verify_mint_adjustment_possible(formulas, precalced_accounts):

Step 06 of 06

Deliver Forecasts the Business Can Trust

Lighthouse doesn't just produce numbers — it explains them. Every forecast output is accompanied by the reasoning, accuracy metrics, and decision trail that finance teams need to act with confidence.

  • Forecasted values by account, product, and region
  • Key drivers identified and ranked per account
  • Forecast accuracy (MAPE, MAE, directional accuracy)
  • Full traceability: which model, which drivers, which lags
  • LLM-powered narrative summaries for stakeholder communication
Why This Matters

Finance teams spend less time defending numbers and more time driving decisions. Explainability reduces the "black box" concern and builds lasting trust in the forecasting system.
